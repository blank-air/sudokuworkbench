When you're stuck

Being stuck almost never means the puzzle needs something clever. Nine times out of ten it means you're asking the wrong question, you're reading stale pencil marks, or you made a mistake twenty moves ago. This is a fixed order to work through — cheapest checks first, so you never go hunting an X-Wing while a hidden single is sitting in plain sight.

First: which kind of stuck is this?

Pick the one that matches and start there. If none quite fit, just begin at Pass 1 — it costs thirty seconds.

The ladder

Work down in order. Each pass is cheaper than the one below it, so finishing a pass with nothing found is still progress — it tells you the answer isn't that kind of answer.

Pass 1

Count the nearly-full units

~30 seconds

Before anything else, look at every row, column and box that has only two or three gaps. Say out loud what's already in it, and the missing digits name themselves.

what you're looking for Any unit with 2–3 empty cells. Nine digits minus what's there equals a very short list of possibilities.
Move on when: you've swept all 27 units once and nothing is forced.
Pass 2

Chase one digit at a time

2–3 minutes

This is the highest-yield scan in Sudoku, and the one most people skip — because staring at an empty cell and asking "what goes here?" feels more natural than picking a digit and asking "where does this go?".

Cell-first makes you juggle nine possibilities at once. Digit-first makes you juggle one. Take the 1s, walk the three bands, then the 2s, and so on. You'll place digits you'd stared past for minutes.

what you're looking for A band of three boxes where your digit already sits in two of them. The rows and columns it occupies knock out most of the third box, often leaving one cell.
Move on when: you've been through all nine digits without a placement.
Pass 3

Only now, pencil everything in

~2 minutes

Notes are a tool for the middle of a puzzle, not the start. Written too early, most of them are noise you'll spend the rest of the game erasing — and a stale mark you forget to erase is the most common cause of a wrong answer.

do it a unit at a time, not a cell at a time Work out one row's missing digits once, then write those marks across all its empty cells. You're reusing a single piece of thinking nine times instead of redoing it nine times.

If you're on the board rather than paper, the "Fill all notes" button does this for you — after which they're yours to edit.

Move on when: every empty cell carries its candidates.
Pass 4

Re-sweep now that you can see

~1 minute

A fresh set of notes almost always exposes moves that weren't visible before. Do both sweeps, in this order:

what you're looking for First, any cell showing a single mark. Then, digit by digit through each unit, any digit with only one home — even if that cell still shows other marks. The second kind hides in plain sight because the cell looks busy.
Move on when: both sweeps come up empty.
Pass 5

Stop placing, start eliminating

~2 minutes

From here on, most moves don't fill a cell — they remove a candidate, which sets up a fill later. This is the mental shift that gets people past the medium barrier.

where to look Boxes that are about half full, and digits you've already placed five or six times. If a digit's options inside a box all land on one line, it's committed to that line — and leaves the rest of it.
Move on when: you've checked each box for a digit confined to one line, and the reverse.
Pass 6

Pairs, then the two-mark map

3+ minutes

Pairs first, because they're easy to see and often cascade. Scan each unit for two cells showing the same two marks.

If pairs give you nothing, light up every cell that's down to exactly two candidates. That set is the raw material for every wing and chain there is, and seeing them together turns a vague hunt into a short list.

Still nothing? Run the mistake check below before reaching for anything more exotic.

The mistake check

Do this whenever a puzzle feels impossible — especially near the end. A grid with an error in it can look exactly like a hard puzzle, and no amount of technique will rescue it.

check

Look for a cell with nothing left

You don't need to re-derive the whole grid. Scan for the two signatures of a broken puzzle: the same digit twice in a row, column or box, or an empty cell with no candidates at all. The second is the more common and the more convincing — it's proof, not a hunch.

on this board Mistake-checking is on by default and flags a wrong digit in red the moment you place it. If you'd rather solve without the safety net you can switch it off — but then run this scan by eye when things stop working.

Traps that keep people stuck

01Asking "what goes here?" forever

Cell-first scanning is the natural instinct and the slowest method. If you've been staring at one region for more than a minute, switch the question to a digit and sweep the whole grid with it. Pass 2 exists for this.

02Trusting stale pencil marks

You placed a 6, but didn't rub the 6 out of the three cells beside it. Every deduction after that is built on sand. If something feels off, rebuild the notes for the unit you're working in rather than trusting what's written.

03Reaching for exotic patterns too early

Swordfish and chains are satisfying to spot and almost never what's actually needed. In practice the overwhelming majority of "impossible" positions come loose to a hidden single or a locked candidate that got skipped. Walk back down the ladder before you climb further up it.

04Assuming the puzzle is hard rather than wrong

Especially with only a handful of cells left. Three empty cells that refuse to resolve is far more likely to be your own error than a grandmaster pattern. Check before you hunt.
